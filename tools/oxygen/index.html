Muscle oxygen explorer

Move capillaries, shut them down or change fibre types in a muscle cross-section and see the predicted oxygen supply respond, with oxygen flux streamlines and capillary domains. Illustrative models that run in your browser.

Drag a capillary, tap it to shut it down, or change a fibre’s type, and watch the predicted oxygen partial pressure (PO₂) across a cross-section of skeletal muscle respond. Oxygen flux streamlines show where the oxygen from each capillary goes, and the Voronoi tessellation of the capillary centres shows each capillary’s domain. The section is synthetic, the parameters come from our published models, and everything runs in your browser. Predictions assume maximal vascular capacity and are illustrative.

Oxygen flux streamlines follow the direction of oxygen flux from each perfused capillary into the tissue. Lines leave each capillary wall at equal steps of flux, so they crowd where more oxygen leaves the wall, and a capillary that supplies more oxygen sends out more lines. They end where the flux falls to zero: at the lowest PO₂ between capillaries, or at the edge of anoxic tissue.

Capillary domains are the Voronoi tessellation of the capillary centres: each domain is the part of the section closer to its capillary than to any other. Domain areas are descriptors of capillary geometry; compare the domain boundaries with where the streamlines from neighbouring capillaries actually meet.

Point at a capillary to see its share of the oxygen leaving all capillaries; point at the tissue to see the area of the domain beneath it.

Tap a fibre to make it

Choose a type, then tap fibres in the section to change them. Type I fibres use the most oxygen and carry the most myoglobin; type IIX the least.

Demand per type is fixed when a tissue is generated, so retyping a fibre changes the tissue's total demand.

Colour map. Viridis, Magma and Cividis change evenly in lightness, so equal steps in PO₂ look like equal steps in colour; Cividis stays readable with colour-vision deficiency. Turbo and Jet are rainbow maps: familiar, but they can create apparent boundaries where PO₂ changes smoothly.

Low-PO₂ threshold. Fibre area below this PO₂ is reported as low. 0.5 mmHg is the hypoxia threshold used in Al-Shammari et al. (2019).

Streamline density. The total number of lines shared among all capillaries: about 150, 300 or 600. Each capillary gets lines in proportion to the oxygen it supplies.

Domains from. All capillaries gives the anatomical domains; Perfused only leaves out shut capillaries, so their tissue is shared among the perfused neighbours.

Export resolution. Each Export PDF button saves that graphic as a PDF at this resolution, 8 cm wide, on a white background. The section export includes the overlays that are switched on.

Model options

Supply · capillaries
Non-perfused capillaries

Shut capillaries stay in place but supply no oxygen: a functional rarefaction. The anatomical capillary counts stay the same, while the perfused values drop.

You can also tap any capillary in the section to shut it or reopen it.

Capillary PO₂

40 mmHg is the maximum. Varied PO₂ spreads capillary values evenly below it, for example where blood has already given up some of its oxygen.

Each capillary keeps its value when moved and is coloured by it.

Capillary wall

Dirichlet holds the capillary wall at the capillary PO₂.

Robin lets oxygen cross the wall at a rate k(Pcap − P), so PO₂ falls across the wall and less oxygen reaches the tissue.

Demand · fibres
Exercise intensity

Demand is set so the tissue-average demand at rest equals the published M₀, keeping the ratios between fibre types, then multiplied by the intensity.

Demand by fibre type

Demand relative to type I is from Al-Shammari, Gaffney & Egginton (2014), Table II (M₀ = 15.7, 13.82 and 7.85 ×10⁻⁵ ml O₂ ml⁻¹ s⁻¹). Absolute values scale with the exercise intensity, and the ratios stay fixed.

Oxygen consumption

Michaelis–Menten consumption slows as PO₂ approaches zero.

Constant consumption continues at the full rate until oxygen runs out, so anoxic regions can form.

Transport in fibres
Myoglobin-facilitated diffusion

Oxygen bound to myoglobin also diffuses inside the fibres, which matters most at low PO₂.

Myoglobin content by fibre type follows Al-Shammari et al. (2014); type I fibres carry the most.

Doubled and Tripled multiply every fibre type's myoglobin content by 2 or 3.

Fibre shape

Realistic fibres have rounded corners that leave pockets where fibres meet, gently curved edges, sizes that differ by type (type II larger), a slight oblique stretch and endomysium of varying thickness.

Switching keeps the same tissue and the baseline, so Change shows the effect of shape alone.

Fibres are counted with an unbiased counting frame: a fibre counts if it lies inside the section or touches only the top or right edge (dashed green), and is excluded if it touches the left or bottom edge (solid red). Things to try

  1. Find a fibre with a dark core and drag a nearby capillary toward it. Watch how far the change spreads, including in the fibres the capillary left.
  2. Press Set as baseline, then move capillaries without changing how many there are. Capillary density and the capillary-to-fibre ratio stay the same, while the predicted PO₂ map and the low-PO₂ area change.
  3. Shut 20% of the capillaries. The anatomical capillary-to-fibre ratio is unchanged, but hypoxic regions appear around the shut ones. Tap individual capillaries to see which ones matter most.
  4. Tap a type I fibre to make it type IIX. Its demand falls, so its PO₂ rises, and neighbours sharing the same capillaries can gain too. Watch the mean PO₂ by fibre type.
  5. Switch from Realistic to Polygons fibres with a baseline set, and compare the means by fibre type: the tissue-wide mean barely moves, but oxygen shifts between fibre types.
  6. Compare the default capillary wall, which resists oxygen exchange (Robin), with fixed capillary PO₂ (Dirichlet). Then set a baseline and switch myoglobin off to see where it helps most.
  7. Follow the streamlines from two neighbouring capillaries to where they meet, and compare that meeting line with the pink domain boundary between them. The two can differ, especially when the capillaries’ PO₂ differs (try Capillary PO₂ 20–40) or when the fibres between them differ in type.
  8. Shut one capillary and watch its neighbours’ streamlines reach into the tissue it used to supply. Switch Domains from to Perfused only to see the matching change in the tessellation.

How it is computed

Oxygen diffuses from the capillaries and is consumed in the fibres. At steady state the PO₂, \(P\), satisfies a reaction–diffusion PDE:

\[\nabla\cdot\left[\,D\alpha\,\nabla P + D_{Mb}C_{Mb}\,\nabla S\,\right] = M\,\frac{P}{P + P_m}, \qquad S = \frac{P}{P + P_{50,Mb}},\]

where the second term on the left, diffusion of oxygen bound to myoglobin, is included only when myoglobin is switched on. Myoglobin is assumed to be in local equilibrium with dissolved oxygen, with saturation \(S\). Consumption and myoglobin are confined to the fibres. Constant consumption is approximated by a very small \(P_m\).

On each perfused capillary wall, either the PO₂ is fixed (Dirichlet), \(P = P_{cap}\), or oxygen crosses the wall at a rate set by its mass transfer coefficient (Robin), \(-\mathbf{n}\cdot D\alpha\nabla P = k\,(P_{cap} - P)\). The outer edge of the section is a no-flux boundary, \(\mathbf{n}\cdot\nabla P = 0\), as for a cut section, and fibres cut by the edge are treated as separate pieces. Myoglobin moves only within fibres.

Demand is set so that the volume average over the whole section at rest equals the published \(M_0\), keeping the published ratios between fibre types, and is then multiplied by the exercise intensity factor. The rates are fixed when a tissue is generated, so retyping a fibre changes the tissue’s total demand. Demand met is the fibres’ total uptake as a share of what they would consume at the full rate.

The equation is solved by finite volumes with nonlinear multigrid. The capillary condition is imposed on each capillary’s true circular wall: grid lines that cross a wall use the exact distance to it, and with a Robin wall the wall PO₂ seen by each neighbouring cell follows the logarithmic profile around a capillary. While you drag a capillary the model is solved on a 2 µm grid; as soon as you let go it is refined on a 1 µm grid, which the readouts, the baseline and PDF exports use. In tests against 0.25–0.5 µm reference solutions, the 1 µm result was within 0.01 mmHg of mean fibre PO₂ with a Dirichlet wall and within about 0.07 mmHg with a Robin wall.

Fibres are counted with an unbiased counting frame: a fibre counts if it lies inside the section or touches only the top or right edge, and not if it touches the left or bottom edge.

Oxygen flux streamlines. Dissolved and myoglobin-bound oxygen both move down the PO₂ gradient, so the flux points along \(-\nabla P\) and the streamlines are curves of steepest descent of PO₂. The gradient is taken from the 1 µm solution by central differences and interpolated bilinearly, and lines are traced with a midpoint (second-order Runge–Kutta) step of half a grid cell. Seeds sit just outside each perfused capillary wall, at equal steps of the outward flux \(\left(D\alpha + D_{Mb}C_{Mb}\,dS/dP\right)\partial P/\partial r\) measured around the wall, so each capillary’s share of the lines matches its share of the oxygen supplied. A line stops where the gradient falls below 0.1% of the median gradient at the capillary walls (a PO₂ minimum, the edge of an anoxic core, or rarely a saddle), or at a perfused lumen, which happens only when a capillary at lower PO₂ takes up oxygen from its neighbours. At the section edge the no-flux condition makes the flux tangential, so lines run along the edge rather than crossing it; lines that end on the edge end at a PO₂ minimum there, which the no-flux condition favours because it leaves out the capillaries beyond the cut. Lines that converge towards the same minimum stop when they come within 0.5 µm of another line, so they are not drawn on top of one another.

In benchmarks over 105 tissues and seven model settings, lines ended with a median 0.2% of the flux they started with. An independent tracer, using a bicubic spline of the same PO₂ field and an adaptive Runge–Kutta integrator, followed the same paths to within a median 0.14 µm.

Capillary domains. The Voronoi tessellation of the capillary centres is computed exactly, by clipping the section square with the perpendicular bisector of each pair of capillaries. Domains cut by the section edge are drawn but left out of the summary statistics.

What the model leaves out

  • The section is two-dimensional: oxygen moves only within the cross-section, not along the fibres or capillaries.
  • Capillary PO₂ is set directly rather than computed from blood flow and the unloading of haemoglobin.
  • Myoglobin is assumed to be in equilibrium with dissolved oxygen everywhere, which cannot hold very close to the fibre boundary.
  • The tissue is synthetic and not fitted to a real section.
  • With a Robin wall, the wall PO₂ seen by each grid cell is taken from the logarithmic profile around a capillary, so variation around the circumference within one grid cell is ignored.
