Curve sketching

An interactive tool for MATH 101, weeks 8–11. See how f′ and f″ shape the graph of f, find critical numbers, local and absolute extreme values, intervals of increase and concavity, inflection points and asymptotes, and check a sketch against them.

← MATH 101

The first and second derivatives tell us the shape of a graph. Where \(f' > 0\), \(f\) is increasing, and where \(f' < 0\) it is decreasing (the Increasing/Decreasing Test). A critical number is a number \(c\) in the domain of \(f\) where \(f'(c) = 0\) or \(f'(c)\) does not exist, and the First Derivative Test decides whether \(f\) has a local maximum or minimum there. Where \(f'' > 0\) the graph is concave upward, where \(f'' < 0\) concave downward, and an inflection point is a point where the concavity changes. On a closed interval, the Closed Interval Method finds the absolute maximum and minimum values. This tool carries out the textbook’s guidelines for sketching a curve and shows \(f\), \(f'\) and \(f''\) together. It supports learning outcomes 8 and 9.

Tick closed interval to restrict f to [a, b] and apply the Closed Interval Method. Without it, the tool studies a range three times as wide as the window and writes its ends as −∞ and ∞, so features far outside the window can be missed. Untick the boxes under Show to sketch first and check afterwards.

Things to try

  1. For \(f(x) = 3x^4 - 16x^3 + 18x^2\) on \([-1, 4]\), the textbook finds a local maximum \(f(1) = 5\), a local minimum \(f(0) = 0\), the absolute maximum \(f(-1) = 37\) and the absolute minimum \(f(3) = -27\). Check each one, and explain why \(f(-1)\) is not a local maximum.
  2. For \(f(x) = x - 2\sin x\) on \([0, 2\pi]\), find the absolute extreme values and compare with the textbook’s \(\pi/3 - \sqrt{3}\) and \(5\pi/3 + \sqrt{3}\).
  3. For \(f(x) = 3x^4 - 4x^3 - 12x^2 + 5\), read off the intervals of increase and decrease from the graph of \(f'\), then check them in the sign chart.
  4. For \(y = x^4 - 4x^3\), \(f'(0) = 0\) but there is no local extremum at 0. What does the sign chart show there? What happens to the concavity?
  5. For \(f(x) = x^{2/3}(6 - x)^{1/3}\), find the critical numbers where \(f'\) does not exist. What does the graph look like at those points?
  6. For \(\dfrac{2x^2}{x^2 - 1}\), \(x e^x\), \(\ln(4 - x^2)\) and \(\dfrac{x^3}{x^2 + 1}\), compare each item in the summary with the textbook’s sketch: domain, asymptotes, extreme values, concavity and inflection points.
  7. Untick the three boxes under Show, sketch \(f'\) and \(f''\) for a function of your choice, and then reveal them.

How it is computed

The derivatives are approximated by central differences, \(f'(x) \approx [f(x + h) - f(x - h)]/(2h)\) and \(f''(x) \approx\) \([f(x + h) - 2f(x) + f(x - h)]/h^2\). The tool samples the range finely and splits it where \(f\) is undefined or jumps; a jump where \(\lvert f\rvert\) grows without bound is a vertical asymptote. In each piece, critical numbers are the zeros of \(f'\) (found as sign changes and refined by bisection) and the points where the one-sided difference quotients disagree or grow without bound. The signs of \(f'\) and \(f''\) between these points give the intervals of increase and concavity, and the First Derivative Test classifies the critical numbers. Horizontal and slant asymptotes come from the values of \(f(x)\) and \(f(x)/x\) for very large \(\lvert x\rvert\). Results are rounded and can be fooled by features smaller than the sampling grid.

The tool runs entirely in your browser; nothing is recorded or sent anywhere. It is a learning aid and is not assessed.
