Proportionality and geometric similarity

An interactive tool for MATH 316, week 4. Test proportionality graphically with the textbook's data, build the vehicular stopping distance model and compare it with rules of thumb, and see how area and volume scale for similar objects.

← MATH 316

Two quantities are proportional, \(y \propto x\), when \(y = kx\) for some constant \(k \neq 0\). So if \(y \propto x^{3}\), the graph of \(y\) against \(x^{3}\) is a straight line through the origin, with slope \(k\). This gives a quick graphical test of a proportionality model: plot \(y\) against the proposed function of \(x\), see whether the points lie close to a line through the origin, and estimate its slope. This tool does that with the data sets in Chapter 2 of the textbook, then puts the stopping distance submodels together and shows how geometric similarity scales areas and volumes. It supports learning outcomes 1 and 2.

In the right-hand panel, click a data point to draw the line through the origin and that point, as the textbook does, or drag anywhere in the panel to tilt the line by eye.

Things to try

  1. Load Kepler’s third law and plot T against R. Do the points lie on a line through the origin? Now plot against R3/2, and click the point for Neptune to get the textbook’s model, T = 0.408R3/2. Use it to answer Problem 13: how long is the year of a planet 4004 million miles from the Sun?
  2. Load stopping distance: reaction distance. Is reaction distance proportional to speed? Estimate the slope and convert it to a response time in seconds (1 mph is 22/15 ft/s). The textbook says these results are “too good”. Why?
  3. Load stopping distance: braking distance and plot it against v, then against v2. The bars show the range of the middle 85% of the observations. Is proportionality to v2 more convincing at low speeds or at high speeds?
  4. Load the bass fishing derby and plot W against l3, then against lg2. Which model has the smaller largest deviation? Which would an angler who caught a fat fish prefer? Try l2g and g3 as well (Problem 9).
  5. Load bird femurs and estimate the slope of W against l3. Predict the weight of the terror bird, whose femur has a circumference of 21 cm. Then load the dinosaur data (Project 7) and predict again for a 210 mm femur. Why do the two predictions differ so much?
  6. Load weight and height and plot W against h3. The points lie close to a straight line, but does it pass through the origin? Turn on the line with an intercept. What does the textbook’s model, W = k1 + k3h2 + k5h3, say the intercept stands for?
  7. For any data set, estimate the slope by eye first, then turn on least squares. How close were you? Is the line through a single chosen point ever the best by either measure in the table? Chapter 3 shows how the least-squares slope is found.

Stopping distance and rules of thumb

The textbook models the total stopping distance as reaction distance plus braking distance. A driver keeps going at speed \(v\) for a response time \(t_r\) before braking, so \(d_r = t_r v\). If the brakes give the same deceleration to any car, the work done by braking equals the loss of kinetic energy, and \(d_b \propto v^{2}\). Together, with \(v\) in mph and distances in feet,

\[d = \tfrac{22}{15}\, t_r\, v + k v^{2},\]

and a response time of 3/4 s with \(k = 0.054\) gives the textbook’s model, \(d = 1.1v + 0.054v^{2}\). The plot compares the model with the observed stopping distances and with three rules for how far to stay behind the car in front.

Observed values from the U.S. Bureau of Public Roads, as given in Table 2.4 of the textbook: each bar spans the middle 85% of the observations, and the dot marks the average.

  1. With the textbook’s values, where does the model lie relative to the bars? At what speed does it start to fall short?
  2. Show the one car length per 10 mph rule. Above what speed does it allow less than the average stopping distance? Do the same for the 2-second rule. The two rules are often said to be the same: are they?
  3. Move the speed and note the trailing time needed to stop within the longest observed distance. Do these times reproduce Table 2.5, and do they support the textbook’s rule of 1, 2, 3 and 4 seconds?
  4. A distracted driver may take 1.5 s to react. How much farther does that driver need to stop at 60 mph? Which rules still leave enough room?
  5. On a wet road the braking constant is larger. Increase it and see which rule fails first.

Geometric similarity

Two objects are geometrically similar when one is a scaled copy of the other: every distance between corresponding points is multiplied by the same factor \(k\). Areas are then multiplied by \(k^{2}\) and volumes by \(k^{3}\), so for similar objects \(S \propto l^{2}\) and \(V \propto l^{3}\) for any characteristic length \(l\). That is the step behind the bass, bird and raindrop models.

  1. Double the scale. By what factors do the lengths, the surface area and the volume change? What happens to the surface area per unit volume, and why does that matter for a small animal keeping warm (Problem 5 of Section 2.3)?
  2. The textbook shows that the terminal velocity of a raindrop is proportional to the one-sixth power of its mass. How much faster does a drop twice as wide fall?
  3. A 12-inch bass weighs about 1 lb. Set the scale to 1.5 to predict the weight of an 18-inch bass, and compare with the textbook’s conversion table.

How it is computed

For data \((x_i, y_i)\) and a proposed model \(y = k\,g(x)\), the tool plots \(y_i\) against \(X_i = g(x_i)\). Your line is \(y = kX\) with the slope you set; clicking a point \((X_j, y_j)\) sets \(k = y_j / X_j\). The least-squares line through the origin has \(k = \sum X_i y_i \big/ \sum X_i^{2}\), and the line with an intercept is the usual least-squares line \(y = a + bX\). The table compares the lines by the sum of squared deviations \(\sum [y_i - f(x_i)]^2\) and the largest absolute deviation \(\max_i \lvert y_i - f(x_i) \rvert\). In the stopping distance plot, a rule that keeps \(t\) seconds behind the car in front allows \(\tfrac{22}{15}\, t\, v\) feet at \(v\) mph, and the trailing time needed is the longest observed stopping distance divided by the speed in feet per second.

The tool runs entirely in your browser; nothing is recorded or sent anywhere. It is a learning aid and is not assessed.
