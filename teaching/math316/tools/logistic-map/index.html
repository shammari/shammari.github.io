The discrete logistic model

An interactive tool for MATH 316, weeks 2–3. Explore equilibria, stability, cycles and chaos in a difference equation with cobweb diagrams.

← MATH 316

A population that grows in discrete generations, and is held back as it nears its limit, can be modelled by the difference equation \(x_{n+1} = r\,x_n (1 - x_n)\). The same model can settle to an equilibrium, oscillate between several values, or never settle at all, depending only on r. This tool shows the cobweb diagram, the sequence itself and a summary of the long-run behaviour for every r. It supports learning outcomes 1 and 11.

The yeast culture

The textbook builds this model from data on a yeast culture growing in a limited space. The change in biomass each hour, \(\Delta p_n = p_{n+1} - p_n\), slows as the biomass approaches a limit of about 665, which suggests the model \(\Delta p_n = k\,(665 - p_n)\,p_n\). Plotting \(\Delta p_n\) against \(p_n (665 - p_n)\) tests the model, and the slope of the line through the origin estimates \(k\).

Yeast biomass each hour from R. Pearl, “The Growth of Population,” Quarterly Review of Biology 2 (1927), as given in the textbook.

The model for any growth rate

The cobweb diagram follows the sequence: up to the curve, across to the diagonal, and repeat. The bottom panel shows, for each r, the values the sequence keeps visiting after it has run for a long time; click it to choose r. Drag the starting point along the horizontal axis of the cobweb diagram to change x0.

Things to try

  1. In the yeast culture, press least-squares k. How close is the estimate to the textbook’s 0.00082? Change M: how sensitive is the fit, and the prediction, to the carrying capacity?
  2. Use the yeast model’s growth rate in the logistic map. The textbook rescales the model to \(a_{n+1} = r(1 - a_n)a_n\) with \(r = 1.546\). Why does the sequence approach its limit without oscillating?
  3. Step through the textbook’s values of r: 1.546, 2.75, 3.25, 3.525, 3.555 and 3.75, starting at 0.1. Describe each long-run behaviour and find it in the bottom panel.
  4. Find the equilibria by solving \(x = r x (1 - x)\). For which values of r is the nonzero equilibrium stable? Check your answer with the tool, and relate it to the slope of the curve where it meets the diagonal.
  5. Set r = 3.83. What do you see, and where is this in the bottom panel?
  6. Set r = 3.75 and turn on compare, which starts a second sequence at x0 + 0.001. How long do the two sequences stay close? What does this mean for long-term prediction?
  7. The continuous logistic equation \(dP/dt = rP(1 - P/K)\) never oscillates. Why can the discrete model oscillate when the continuous one cannot?

How it is computed

The sequence is computed directly from the difference equation. The equilibria are \(x^* = 0\) and \(x^* = 1 - 1/r\), and an equilibrium is stable when \(\lvert f'(x^*) \rvert < 1\), where \(f(x) = r x (1 - x)\). To find the long-run behaviour, the tool discards the first 1000 terms and then looks for a repeating cycle of length up to 64. The textbook form \(\Delta p_n = k\,p_n (M - p_n)\) becomes this model after the change of variable \(x_n = k p_n / (1 + kM)\), with \(r = 1 + kM\); for the yeast culture, \(k = 0.00082\) and \(M = 665\) give \(r = 1.5453\), which the textbook rounds to 1.546.

The tool runs entirely in your browser; nothing is recorded or sent anywhere. It is a learning aid and is not assessed.
